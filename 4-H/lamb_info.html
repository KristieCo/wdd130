Shearing: I have had a hard time the last few years with finding someone to shear lambs. The guy that I absolutely love having do it has not been available to help me the last few years. So, I have been asking other leaders to see what they do. It sounds like a lot of the kids will shear their own lambs. Some have talked about how their club has clippers and each family has their own blades and they take turns with them. I am leaning towards that this year. I can also find names of people who could potentially shear sheep and let you take care of your family or you are welcome to use our tools. When we get closer, we can talk as well. You all may want to shear them a time or two this summer anyway to make sure that they are staying cool enough to eat and gain their weight as well.

Feeding instructions for lambs: You can feed your lambs, lamb friendly minerals if you would like. We do not always do this, but it won't hurt, just be sure that it is sheep friendly. Cooper is toxic to lambs, so make sure that whatever minerals you give your lambs are lamb safe. When changing feed for your lamb, do so gradually so that their stomach can adjust. If you change feed and your lamb has diarrhea, you may want to consider giving them Kao pectate or kaolin pectin several times a day until it stops or call the vet if you have concerns. Upper Valley Vet is a great resource. Make sure that they always have access to clean, cool water. If they are not drinking, eating, excessively coughing, or look like they are not feeling well (head down all of the time and not moving much), also call the vet, especially if you do not see improvement soon. We want the lambs to be eating about 3% of their body weight in grain by the time we reach our first weigh-in in June. They will need to also have hay or grass to keep their stomachs moving and them pooping. So, something to watch out for. We want to avoid fat hay bellies which come from too much hay. (If I am doing my math right for a 75-pound lamb they should be eating about 2.25 pounds of grain a day or about 1.12 pounds morning and night. With of course their football size or so wad of hay each day. Of course, these numbers will change as they grow so weighing things can help a lot.)

Weighing lambs and feed: You should have their weight from when you buy them and can do the math and weigh grain if you would like. Your lambs should be gaining between ½ to ¾ pounds a day so you can calculate what you think they weigh, or measure them with a tape measure and have an estimated weight, (measure heart girth and body length from front of chest to tail. The calculation is weight = (heart girth X heart girth X body length) / 300) or you can use our hanging scale and get a more accurate weight. You are welcome to use it anytime. We are planning to have club weigh-ins every two weeks or so throughout the summer at the fair grounds. If you feel that one or two of your lambs are not eating as much as they should or being pushed out, you may want to consider feeding them separately, but so they can still see each other. They love to be together all of the time and get stressed if they are not together. There is always a little pig in the group and someone who gets pushed out, so keep an eye on how they look so that you can know if you need to make changes. After first weigh in and they are about to 3% their body weight of grain (1 – 1 ½ morning and night) you can reduce their hay or grass intake to about a football size bunch per lamb each day. The hay or grass mix is to help keep their digestive tract moving. If they appear constipated, then give them some more hay and if they get diarrhea, they could need less grain for a bit. If they do get diarrhea, they are safe to have Kao pectate or kaolin pectin which you can get at valley wide or CAL Ranch. But if the diarrhea does not clear up in a few days, you can call the vet after backing off on the grain and giving them more hay. Always make sure that they have clean, fresh water all of the time.

Bloat: If they eat too fast and or start foaming out of the mouth, they could be bloating and you will need to get them to burp by reaching under their tummy and lifting up several times to get as much air out of their stomachs as possible. Also use a syringe and give them some baking soda and water mixed together. This will help the acid build up. You can give them 20 to 30 ml or more and do it a few times over the next hour or so and keep an eye on them. If caught early, it can be fixed.

Prolapse: If your lamb starts to cough and I mean an excessive amount of coughing not just once or twice a day but continually, this can cause other problems so take them to see the vet for medicine. If their feed is moldy or very dry and there is a lot of dust, this can cause coughing so wet things down if needed to keep a lot of dust gone. If you do notice your lamb have body tissue, come out of its bottom while baaing or coughing, it would be good to have someone come and look to see if they should go to the vet. If prolapse is caught early and fixed, there is a good chance that they can still show at the fair. But if not taken care of soon enough, they will not be allowed at the fair grounds. Bridger’s lamb that prolapsed a few years ago had a perfect storm. Moldy feed so she started to cough a little bit, then we switched where they were kept and she got a lot of new, fresh food which gave her diarrhea, and once the new vegetation was gone things got dry and there was a lot of dust which made her coughing even worse. She coughed all night which made things worse. She ended up getting stitched and taken care of too late and it left a bulge so she could not even go to the fair. So, watch your lambs and get treatment when needed.

Working with your lambs: Have kids put them on their halters daily if possible so that they can get used to it. They can try walking them or tying them to something and touching them a lot. Our goal is to have the lambs used to being touched and not pull or jump when on their halter or touched. We will talk a lot more about this as the summer gets going. Walking them lots in the heat of the day is not always good and they can lose rather than gain muscle and weight. So, working with them when it is cooler is better and short harder walks are better than easy long walks. They described it as a weight lifter verses a marathoner if that makes sense. We want them to gain muscle and be lean, but not lean without any muscle. I have heard of a 3 on, 4 off schedule which they walk thier lamb 3 days a week and 4 days a week they tie them up and touch them, put them on the stand, wash them, and or brush out their leg hair. Just spend time with them and not always walking them. We try and work with animals every evening once it starts to cool down and walk or spend time with them touching them. Find what works best for you.

Grumpy Lambs: Some of our lambs are getting a little grumpier with kids and fighting them more. Hopefully this will change as we more consistently work with them. If need be, they do not like to be put on their backs so you can flip them over if needed or halter and tie them to a fence or something so that they know when they pull, they will not go anywhere. But of course, keep an eye on them so they do not hurt themselves. Keep working hard, it will pay off.

Lamb not gaining or acting right: Do they look like they are eating and drinking well? Are they acting alright? Make sure that they have access to cool, clean water.They could not be feeling well and need dewormed or their ear could be hurting or sore from the tag, or they could just be too warm and need sheared? Many things can bother them. If they are not feeling well, they will not be very good when working with them. If their ear has scabs and yuck by their tags, hydrogen peroxide and antibiotic ointment can help. Just a reminder to you all to watch and see who is eating and drinking and what their poop looks like. Lambs can have pellets, but they also have pellets that stick together. Just make sure that they are not staining to poop or that they do not have diarrhea. They can have poop that is not pellets and just a log and softer, but we do not want runny or super hard. Sorry, kind of gross but important. Watch them and make sure that they also have plenty of clean, cool water if possible and that they are not too lethargic because that is another sign that things may not be going well. Of course, they are lazy when it is hot just like us. When it cools down you can also have a better idea.
